\section{Introduction}

Operational Modal Analysis (OMA) encompasses a set of techniques developed to calculate and visualize modal vibrations of structures from real-world measurements. In this article we explore OMA techniques recently added to the Senmos cloud platform, and the ways in which they can be used to gain a better understanding of how structures like bridges, chimneys and wind turbines interact with external forces. 

Every structure has a set of natural frequencies which it tends to vibrate at when excited by an outside force. For instance, if a swing is lifted upwards and then let go, it swings back and forth at a certain rate which is determined by the length of its ropes. The same phenomenon is at play with more complex structures, although there is no easy way to calculate the natural frequencies based on physical characteristics of these structures. For each natural frequency $f_k$, there is also an associated mode shape $\bm{\phi}_k$ which dictates the relative amplitude and phase (encoded as a complex number) at which each measurement point on a structure oscillates. A damping ratio $\zeta_k$ quantifies how quickly oscillation in a specific mode decays, relative to the natural frequency $f_k$. For instance, a damping ratio of $0.01$ indicates that after one oscillation, the amplitude has decreased by $0.01 \times 2\pi \approx 6.3\%$.

An OMA technique is an algorithm which converts measurement signals gathered from arrays of accelerometers placed at various points on a structure into natural frequencies, damping modes, and mode shapes (see Figure~\ref{fig:oma-schematic}).

The variety of external forces which can interact with a structure greatly complicates the task of separating physical characteristics like natural frequencies from temporary forced vibrations caused by these forces. Due to this, every OMA algorithm requires some form of calibration to give accurate results. For instance, algorithms based on frequency-domain decomposition (FDD) produce transient natural frequencies under most circumstances. Fortunately, Senmos exposes methods by which even a non-expert can quickly identify which natural frequencies produced are transient and which are genuine.

\subsection{FDD-Based Methods}\label{sec:fdd}

FDD-based methods were introduced in the early 2000s~\cite{Brincker2000OutputonlyMA,Brincker2001DampingEB} and operate on the frequency domain representation of measurement signals, typically the power spectral density (PSD) matrix $\mathbf{G}_{yy}\left(\omega \right)$ which collects all cross-spectral power densities.

Under mild conditions, the input PSD matrix ${\mathbf{G}}_{\mathrm{xx}}\left(\omega \right)$ can be related to the output PSD matrix ${\mathbf{G}}_{yy}\left(\omega \right)$ (corresponding to the actual sensor measurements) by the following equation:
\[
\mathbf{G}_{yy}\left(\omega \right) = 
\mathbf{H}^{\dagger}\left(\omega \right)\mathbf{G}_{xx}\left(\omega \right)\mathbf{H}\left(\omega \right)
\]
where $\mathbf{H}(\omega)$ is the unknown Frequency Response Function (FRF) matrix which determines the system-environment interaction.

At a given frequency $\omega$, only 1-2 modes will typically contribute significantly to the system output. Assuming that external forces have a PSD corresponding to white noise, one can derive the following equation~\cite{Brincker2000OutputonlyMA}: 
\[
\bar{G}_{yy}(\omega)
=
\sum_{k}
\left(
\frac{d_k \bm{\phi}_k \bm{\phi}_k^{T}}{j\omega-\lambda_k}
+
\frac{\bar{d}_k \bar{\bm{\phi}}_k \bar{\bm{\phi}_k^{T}}}
{j\omega-\bar{\lambda}_k}
\right)
\]
where $\lambda_k = 2\pi f_k\left(-\zeta_k + j\right)$, $d_k$ determines the relative contribution of each mode, and the sum is over all significant modes.

Supposing that natural frequencies can be clearly distinguished, it is easy to see that $\bar{G}_{yy}(\omega) \to \frac{d_k \bm{\phi}_k \bm{\phi}_k^{T}}{2\pi f_k\zeta_k}$ as $\omega$ approaches some natural frequency (measured in angular units) $2\pi f_k$. In this case $\bm{\phi}_k$ will correspond to the largest left singular vector of $\bar{G}_{yy}(\omega)$. Furthermore, since $\bar{G}_{yy}(\omega)$ is approximately a rank-1 matrix under these conditions, the corresponding singular value (SV) is much larger than the rest of the spectrum. By a similar analysis, one can show that if two (or more) natural frequencies are close to one another, the corresponding mode shapes are proportional to the largest two left singular vectors of $\bar{G}_{yy}(\omega)$ and the corresponding singular values are much larger than the rest of the spectrum.

FDD based methods discretize the frequency spectrum and compute the singular-value decomposition (SVD) at each point. A modal parameter estimation (MPE) plot is then produced, depicting the first few largest singular values (normalized to the $[0,1]$ range relative to the maximum singular value) against the frequency $f$. The first and second scenarios highlighted above will be clearly visible in such a plot as a peak reaching $\approx 1.0$ in the first SV and large peaks in the first and second SVs which together sum to $\approx 1.0$ respectively.

Figure~\ref{fig:sv-plot} shows an MPE plot for our running example of a wind turbine, depicting the peak observed at an isolated natural frequency.

\subsection{SSI-Based Methods}\label{sec:ssi}

SSI-based methods can operate on both the time and frequency domains of a system. In this section we focus on SSI-COV, which operates on the time domain and is implemented in Senmos. 

SSI-based methods model the (discrete-time) dynamics of the system using the two linear equations:
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{t} + \mathbf{w}_t \\
    \mathbf{y}_{t+1} = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}_{t}
\end{align*}
where $\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{y}_t$ are the system state and measurements collected at time $t$ respectively, while $\mathbf{w}_t, \mathbf{v}_t$ are random noise signals caused by external forces and limited measurement precision respectively. They are typically assumed to be white noise.
